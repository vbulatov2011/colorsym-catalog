Cosets, Orbits, and Color Symmetry

A short undergraduate tutorial on how a subgroup H of a symmetry group G generates colored patterns — worked out completely on the simplest non-normal example, the index-3 subgroup *33/*[3] of the dihedral group D3.

1. From symmetry groups to colored patterns

Suppose a geometric pattern is built by taking a single asymmetric motif inside a fundamental domain F and repeating it under every symmetry of a group G. Because the motif has no symmetries of its own, the copies g(F) are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements g ∈ G. Coloring the pattern with n colors therefore means partitioning the group G into n disjoint subsets.

Given any subgroup H ≤ G of finite index [G : H] = n, algebra offers two natural ways to partition G into n equal-sized pieces of size |H|: into the cosets Hg and into the orbits fH. When H is normal in G, both partitions are identical. When H is not normal, they come apart — revealing the geometric meaning of normality, conjugacy, and group actions in a single glance.

Composition convention

Throughout this catalog, group elements act left to right: (xy)(p) = y(x(p)) — apply x first, then y. Consequently, applying a symmetry x ∈ G to the whole plane carries the tile w(F) to (wx)(F) (multiplication on the right). To avoid confusion between algebraic and geometric left/right labels, the catalog refers only to cosets Hg and orbits fH.

2. Our running example: the dihedral group G = *33 ≅ D3

The smallest non-abelian group is the dihedral group D3 ≅ S3 of order 6 — the symmetries of an equilateral triangle, written *33 in Conway’s orbifold notation (and listed under Axial groups › *33). It is generated by two mirror reflections meeting at 60°:

G = *33 = ⟨ a, b  |  a2 = b2 = (ab)3 = e ⟩
  • a is reflection across the horizontal axis (0°).
  • b is reflection across the 60° line.
  • Their product ab (first a, then b) is a +120° counterclockwise rotation; ba is a −120° clockwise rotation; and bab = aba is reflection across the 120° line.

Applying the 6 elements of G to the base sector F = [0°, 60°] (centered at 30°) labels all six wedges around the origin:

3. A non-normal index-3 subgroup: H = {e, a}

Choose the subgroup H = ⟨a⟩ = {e, a} ≅ C2 generated by the single horizontal reflection a. Its order is |H| = 2, so its index is n = [G : H] = 6 / 2 = 3, giving the 3-color group *33/*[3].

Is H normal in G? Conjugate H by b:

b−1Hb = bHb = { e, bab }  ≠  { e, a } = H.

So H is not normal. In fact, its normalizer is NG(H) = H, which means H has K = [G : NG(H)] = 3 distinct conjugate subgroups in G (the three reflections {e, a}, {e, b}, and {e, bab}). Equivalently, H has 3 inequivalent placements inside G.

4. Coloring by cosets (.1, .2, .3) — perfect colorings

Partition G into the three cosets Hg0, Hg1, Hg2 using the transversal g0 = e, g1 = b, g2 = bab, and assign one color to each coset:

  • Color 0 (cream): H = {e, a} — wedges +30°, −30° (right 120° sector).
  • Color 1 (amber): Hb = {b, ab} — wedges +90°, +150° (upper-left 120° sector).
  • Color 2 (espresso): Hbab = {bab, ba} — wedges −150°, −90° (lower-left 120° sector).

Under right action, every symmetry x ∈ G carries each coset Hgj cleanly onto another single coset (Hgj)x = H(gjx). Thus every symmetry of the uncolored pattern induces a well-defined color permutation — a group homomorphism φ : G → Sn. In our example, φ(a) = (1 2) (fixes cream, swaps amber ↔ espresso), φ(b) = (0 1) (swaps cream ↔ amber, fixes espresso), and φ(ab) = (0 1 2) cycles all three colors. The subgroup H = {g ∈ G : φ(g)(0) = 0} is precisely the stabilizer of color 0.

Theorem (van der Waerden & Burckhardt, 1961)

Let G be a symmetry group and let a transitive n-color group be an action of G on n colors via a transitive color-permutation homomorphism φ : G → Sn (meaning some symmetry in G maps any color to any other). Then:

  1. Finite-index subgroups: The stabilizer H = {g ∈ G : φ(g)(0) = 0} of one chosen color is a subgroup of finite index [G : H] = n, the n color classes correspond to the n cosets Hgj, and φ is the permutation action of G on those cosets. Conversely, every subgroup H ≤ G of finite index n defines a transitive n-color group in this way.
  2. Classification up to color relabeling: Two transitive n-colorings of G differ only by permuting (relabeling) the n colors if and only if their stabilizer subgroups are conjugate in G (H′ = g−1Hg). Classifying n-color groups therefore reduces to enumerating conjugacy classes of index-n subgroups of G — exactly what this catalog lists.
  3. Color-permutation group & normal core: The symmetries fixing every color simultaneously form the kernel ker(φ) = ∩g∈G g−1Hg (the normal core of H in G), and by the First Isomorphism Theorem the group of induced color permutations is Im(φ) ≅ G / ker(φ).

Applying this to *33/*[3], the three conjugates of H = {e, a} are {e, a}, {e, b}, and {e, bab}, whose intersection is trivial: ker(φ) = {e}. Only the identity fixes all three colors, so the color-permutation group is Im(φ) ≅ D3 ≅ S3 of order 6 — strictly larger than the number of colors (n = 3) precisely because H is not normal.

Why are there three distinct coset colorings (.1, .2, .3)?

In the coloring .1 above, we placed the base motif inside f0(F) = F and colored tile w(F) by which coset Hgj contains w. What if we instead start from one of the other two cells of H’s fundamental domain — f1 = b or f2 = ab — and color each set fiHgj with color j? Because right multiplication still sends (fiHgj)x = fiH(gjx), every column i is a perfect 3-coloring with the exact same permutation action φ(a) = (1 2), φ(b) = (0 1) — yet geometrically the three patterns are completely different!

In general, two cells fi and fk yield the same partition (up to relabeling colors) if and only if fi and fk lie in the same coset of the normalizer NG(H). Hence the number of genuinely distinct coset colorings is always the placement count K = [G : NG(H)] (here K = 3).

5. Coloring by orbits (.o)

Instead of grouping by cosets Hgj, we can take the three cells f0 = e, f1 = b, f2 = ab of H’s fundamental domain FH and give each H-orbit fiH its own color:

  • Orbit 0 (cream): eH = {e, a} — wedges +30°, −30° (right pair).
  • Orbit 1 (amber): bH = {b, ba} — wedges +90°, −90° (vertical hourglass).
  • Orbit 2 (espresso): abH = {ab, bab} — wedges +150°, −150° (left pair).
Algebraic criterion for normality

Coloring by orbits (.o) and coloring by cosets (.1) define the same partition if and only if H ⊴ G is a normal subgroup (gH = Hg for all g ∈ G). Whenever H is non-normal, .o and .1 agree on color 0 (the subgroup H itself) and must disagree on at least one other color.

Contrast: the normal index-2 subgroup *33/33[2]

Take instead the rotational subgroup H = {e, ab, ba} ≅ C3 of index 2. Every index-2 subgroup is automatically normal (H ⊴ G), so gH = Hg for all g ∈ G: cosets and orbits coincide (.o = .1) and there is only K = 1 distinct 2-coloring (cream = the three rotated wedges, amber = the three reflected wedges). By van der Waerden’s theorem, its normal core is ker(φ) = H ≅ C3 (every rotation fixes both colors), so the color-permutation group is simply the quotient Im(φ) ≅ G / H ≅ C2 of order 2. Open entry →

7. References & further reading

Accessible introductions & textbooks

  • J. H. Conway, H. Burgiel, and C. Goodman-Strauss, The Symmetries of Things, A K Peters / CRC Press (2008). Introduces Conway’s orbifold notation used throughout this catalog and explains symmetry groups across Euclidean, spherical, and hyperbolic geometries.
  • B. Grünbaum and G. C. Shephard, Tilings and Patterns, W. H. Freeman (1987; 2nd ed. Dover, 2016), Chapter 8: “Colored Patterns.” Clear geometric treatment of perfect colorings and color groups of periodic tilings.
  • D. Schattschneider, Visions of Symmetry: Notebooks, Periodic Drawings, and Related Work of M. C. Escher, W. H. Freeman (1990; 2nd ed. Harry N. Abrams, 2004). Classic visual exploration of Escher’s color-symmetry drawings and underlying group theory.
  • M. Senechal, “Coloring Symmetrical Objects Symmetrically,” Mathematics Magazine 56(1), 3–16 (1983). An ideal undergraduate article connecting subgroups, cosets, and colored polyhedra/patterns.
  • H. S. M. Coxeter, “A Simple Introduction to Colored Symmetry,” International Journal of Quantum Chemistry 31(3), 455–461 (1987). Brief, concrete introduction using reflection groups and Escher’s circle-limit prints.

Algebraic theory & classification of color groups

  • B. L. van der Waerden and J. J. Burckhardt, “Farbgruppen,” Zeitschrift für Kristallographie 115(3–4), 231–234 (1961). The founding theorem classifying transitive n-color groups via color-permutation homomorphisms φ : G → Sn and conjugacy classes of finite-index subgroups H ≤ G ([G : H] = n).
  • T. W. Wieting, The Mathematical Theory of Chromatic Plane Ornaments, Pure and Applied Mathematics 71, Marcel Dekker (1982). Complete classification of n-color wallpaper groups up to 60 colors; forms the algorithmic foundation of SymmHub’s subgroup engine.
  • R. L. E. Schwarzenberger, “Colour Symmetry,” Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society 16(3), 209–240 (1984). Comprehensive mathematical survey of color groups, equivalence relations, and counting theorems.
  • M. Senechal, “Color Groups,” Discrete Applied Mathematics 1(1–2), 51–73 (1979); and “Color Symmetry,” Computers & Mathematics with Applications 16(5–8), 545–553 (1988). Foundational definitions of color symmetry via transitive permutation representations.
  • R. L. Roth, “Color Symmetry and Group Theory,” Discrete Mathematics 38(2–3), 273–296 (1982). Systematic group-theoretic formulation in terms of subgroup chains K ≤ H ≤ G.
  • J. D. Jarratt and R. L. E. Schwarzenberger, “Coloured Plane Groups,” Acta Crystallographica A36, 884–888 (1980). Enumeration of colored wallpaper groups by number of colors.

Spherical, hyperbolic & non-perfect colorings

  • M. Senechal, “Color Symmetry and Colored Polyhedra,” Acta Crystallographica A39, 505–511 (1983). Classification of colorings of the finite spherical and polyhedral point groups.
  • M. L. A. N. De Las Peñas, R. P. Felix, and G. R. Laigo, “Colorings of Hyperbolic Plane Crystallographic Patterns,” Zeitschrift für Kristallographie 221(10), 665–672 (2006). Extends subgroup-based color symmetry to hyperbolic triangle and quadrilateral groups.
  • M. L. A. N. De Las Peñas, R. P. Felix, and E. D. B. Provido, “On the Construction of Colored Plane Crystallographic Patterns,” and R. P. Felix, “Enumerating and Identifying Semiperfect Colorings” (2008). Studies non-perfect and semiperfect colorings arising when symmetries preserve only part of a color partition.
  • D. Frettlöh, “Counting Perfect Colourings of Plane Regular Tilings,” Zeitschrift für Kristallographie 223(11–12), 773–776 (2008). Enumeration of perfect colorings of regular Euclidean and hyperbolic tilings.
